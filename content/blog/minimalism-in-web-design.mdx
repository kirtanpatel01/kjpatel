Minimalism is often misunderstood as simply making a website look clean.

A white background, a few lines of text, and plenty of empty space do not automatically make a design minimalist. True minimalism is about **intentional reduction**—removing everything that does not contribute to the experience and giving the remaining elements enough space to matter.

For the first experiment in my design exploration series, I wanted to understand minimalism by actually building a website around its principles rather than simply reading about them.

The result was **[ATELIER NØR](https://atelier-nor.kjpatel.workers.dev/)**, a fictional architecture studio based in Copenhagen ([live site](https://atelier-nor.kjpatel.workers.dev/)).

## The Idea Behind ATELIER NØR

Architecture felt like a natural subject for exploring minimalism.

Buildings already depend heavily on proportion, space, material, light, and structure. Those same principles translate surprisingly well into digital interfaces.

Instead of creating another generic portfolio, I imagined ATELIER NØR as an independent architecture studio whose work focuses on quiet, considered spaces.

The central statement became:

> **Spaces with purpose.**

That idea influenced everything from the typography and grid to the photography and content.

## Designing With Less

The biggest challenge with minimalism is knowing what to remove.

It is easy to add things to a website:

- another button
- another card
- another animation
- another color
- another icon
- another section

It is much harder to decide that something isn't necessary.

For ATELIER NØR, I deliberately avoided many of the patterns that have become common in modern web design.

There are no oversized CTA buttons, floating cards, gradients, glass effects, decorative blobs, or excessive shadows.

The interface relies primarily on four things:

**Typography.**

**Whitespace.**

**Grid.**

**Photography.**

Everything else has a supporting role.

## Whitespace Is Not Empty

One of the most important lessons from this experiment was that whitespace isn't simply the absence of content.

It creates hierarchy.

A large gap between two projects tells the viewer that they are separate pieces of work. Space around a headline gives it importance. A restrained amount of information makes individual details easier to notice.

Instead of trying to fill the viewport, the design allows parts of the page to breathe.

This becomes particularly important in an architecture portfolio because the photographs already contain a lot of visual information.

The interface doesn't need to compete with them.

## Typography as the Interface

Typography became one of the primary visual elements of the site.

I used **Instrument Serif** for the large editorial statements and **Inter** for supporting information, navigation, and metadata.

The contrast is subtle but effective.

Large serif typography gives the site a more expressive architectural-editorial character, while the sans-serif keeps practical information precise and readable.

Rather than relying on cards or decorative components to establish hierarchy, the typography does most of that work.

For example:

![Spaces with purpose](/images/blog/assets/spaces-with-purpose.png)

doesn't need a container, illustration, gradient, or oversized button around it.

The typography and surrounding space are enough.

## Building the Project Section

The Selected Work section contains four fictional projects:

- House No. 17
- North Harbour House
- Stillness Pavilion
- Sønder House

Instead of placing them into a conventional grid of identical cards, each project uses a slightly different composition.

One project places the image on the right.

Another reverses the arrangement.

Another introduces the photograph before the project information.

The final project returns to a quieter asymmetric layout.

The underlying grid remains consistent, but the composition changes.

This creates rhythm without introducing unnecessary decoration.

## Photography Matters

Minimalism doesn't mean that every element has to be visually boring.

In fact, when there are fewer interface elements, photography becomes even more important.

The architectural photographs were chosen to communicate the characteristics of each project rather than simply fill an image slot.

A courtyard residence communicates something different from an adaptive-reuse warehouse or a small pavilion in a park.

The goal was to make the imagery feel like part of the studio's architectural language rather than generic stock photography.

## Restraint in Interaction

The same philosophy applies to interaction.

Hover states exist, but they are deliberately subtle.

Project titles transition toward a muted warm-brown accent, while images scale very slightly when hovered.

There are no dramatic cursor effects or elaborate transitions.

The interaction should be noticed only after the user interacts with the interface.

That restraint helps keep the focus on the architecture.

## What I Learned

Building the site made one thing particularly clear:

**Minimalism is not about having less content. It's about having less unnecessary content.**

A minimalist interface can still contain:

- detailed project descriptions
- multiple images
- navigation
- metadata
- studio information
- contact information

The difference is that each element has a reason to exist.

The challenge isn't removing everything.

It's deciding what deserves to remain.

## Final Thoughts

ATELIER NØR was the first experiment in my exploration of different web design styles.

It taught me to think about design less as a collection of components and more as a system of decisions.

The most useful question throughout the process became:

> **Does this element need to be here?**

If the answer was no, it went away.

That principle sounds simple, but applying it consistently is much harder than it appears.

And that's probably the most interesting thing about minimalism.

**The fewer elements you have, the more responsibility each element carries.**
